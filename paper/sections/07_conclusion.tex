\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced the Split-Landscape Discrepancy, a no-candidate-retraining metric
that compares full-data and surrogate GBDT split-gain landscapes in one
histogram pass, and used it to study 17 condensation methods across 26 tabular
datasets. Method-level split-regret correlates with downstream AUROC at
Spearman $\rho=-0.87$ in the small-to-mid-size diagnostic grid, showing that the
landscape comparison is a useful screening and family-ranking tool before any
candidate is retrained. The large-scale audit qualifies the claim: when
dominant split margins saturate, root split-regret becomes a rejection filter
rather than a fine-grained ranker.

The central finding overturns a natural intuition: a selector with a provable
$(1-1/e)$ coverage guarantee is still among the weakest downstream performers
in the audit. Breadth of split-threshold coverage is not a proxy for predictive
fidelity, because AUROC depends on preserving the gain-weighted structure of
the dominant splits, not on counting reproduced thresholds. The reference-chain
decomposition (\cref{eq:decomp}) operationalises this distinction: coverage
failure is a structure failure, not a leaf failure, and each failure mode has a
distinct remedy, making the decomposition a practical debugging tool.

The diagnostic reaches its limits at regression tasks, where structure mismatch
dominates and small condensed sets cannot overcome it; at HPO transfer, where
configuration rankings change even when final AUROC is similar; and at very
large scale, where reasonable condensers clear the dominant root margins and
random sampling becomes difficult to beat. Within those boundaries, the
workflow is simple: compute split-regret to screen out poor candidates, prefer
low-regret methods when margins are not saturated, and validate
hyperparameter and regression decisions on full data. We release the benchmark
and implementation to support future evaluations of new condensation methods.
